Understanding hadrons from first principles.

We use lattice QCD to study unstable hadrons such as the ρ meson and the Δ baryon, the three-dimensional structure of the nucleon, and nucleon form factors at high momentum transfer.

About

Where most of the visible mass comes from.

Most of the mass of atoms comes from the nucleons, whose masses are far greater than those of their constituent quarks. This mass arises dynamically from the non-perturbative nature of the strong nuclear force, described by Quantum Chromodynamics (QCD). Because quarks are confined and do not exist as asymptotic states, a hadron is qualitatively very different from a hydrogen atom or positronium in QED, and perturbation theory in the coupling cannot describe hadrons at low energies.

The regularization of QCD on a space-time lattice, first proposed by K. G. Wilson in 1974, is the only reliable ab initio method for studying these non-perturbative phenomena. Thanks to exponential growth in computing resources and continual algorithmic improvements, lattice QCD has become a powerful and precise tool for computing the properties of hadrons, which is needed to interpret experiments, resolve puzzles such as the origin of the nucleon spin, and understand the quark structure of the nucleon and its excitations.

Current research

Our projects

Three directions in hadronic physics, studied with lattice QCD.

01

The structure of unstable hadrons

Many hadrons decay through the strong interaction into lighter hadrons. One of these is the Δ baryon, which decays into a nucleon and a pion. It plays an important role in nuclear physics and has been studied extensively at Jefferson Lab. Earlier lattice calculations of its structure neglected the strong decay and were therefore only approximate. Recent theoretical breakthroughs make exact calculations possible. We are first testing these methods on processes involving the ρ meson, which decays into two pions: radiative transitions of the ρ, and weak decays of B and D mesons into ρ mesons and leptons, which may help resolve a current puzzle in particle physics.

02

The three-dimensional structure of the nucleon

Transverse momentum-dependent parton distribution functions (TMDs) describe the momentum distribution of quarks in the nucleon, complementing what is known about their spatial distribution. They are a focus of experiments at the 12 GeV Jefferson Lab and the planned Electron-Ion Collider. We have developed a scheme for calculating TMDs in lattice QCD and are extending it to observables as a function of the longitudinal momentum fraction, to direct calculations of standard parton distribution functions, and to quark orbital angular momentum, a key part of the origin of the nucleon spin.

03

Nucleon form factors at high momentum transfer

At short distances, the electromagnetic structure of the proton and neutron is expected to show scaling features predicted by perturbative QCD. We are computing nucleon electric and magnetic form factors at momentum transfers up to 12 GeV2 on a fine lattice (a = 0.076 fm), which keeps discretization errors under control. This work is a theoretical counterpart to experiments at the upgraded Jefferson Lab that measure elastic form factors up to 18 GeV2.

Phys. Rev. Lett. 134, 161901 (2025)

B → ρℓν̄ resonance form factors from B → ππℓν̄

The first lattice QCD calculation of the B → ρ form factors in which the ρ is treated properly as a resonance in P-wave ππ scattering. The B → ππ form factors are computed as a function of momentum transfer and ππ invariant mass using the Lellouch-Lüscher formalism, then analytically continued to the ρ pole. The decay is a promising route to |Vub| and to the exclusive-inclusive discrepancy.

Quark orbital angular momentum from a twist-3 generalized parton distribution

Quark orbital angular momentum in the proton from the second Mellin moment of the twist-3 GPD Ẽ2T in the forward limit, using a direct derivative method. The result is consistent with earlier evaluations via generalized TMDs and Ji's sum rule.
